How to make a family tree — and get it printed properly

Most of the work is not the drawing. It is asking, while there is still somebody to ask, and writing down what you are told before it goes.

This is the guide I wanted when I started: no accounts, no subscriptions, no talk of DNA kits. Just what to write down, in what order, and how to end up with something worth hanging on a wall. It is written around Heirloom, which is free and runs in your browser, but the first half is true whatever you use.

1. Start with yourself, not with the oldest ancestor

Everyone begins by trying to remember a great-grandfather's brother and stalls inside ten minutes. Put your own name down, then your parents, then their parents, then outwards to brothers, sisters, husbands and wives. A tree grows from somebody, and that somebody may as well be you.

Three things are worth more than any others, and all three are ordinary:

  • Full names, including the name someone was born with. A woman who married is in half the records under a name nobody in the family uses any more.
  • Years. Not full dates — years. About 1910 is worth having. Nothing is not.
  • Places, and what people did. Nagpur, railway clerk is how a person is found again in a register fifty years from now, and it is also the line that makes a stranger on the chart into somebody's grandfather.

2. Ask the oldest person in the family first

This is the part with a clock on it. Sit down with whoever remembers most, and do not arrive with a form. Ask who their parents' brothers and sisters were. Ask who married whom, and roughly when. Ask what the house was like and who lived in it. The names come out sideways, in the middle of a story.

Record it rather than writing it. In Heirloom there is a microphone beside the name, place and occupation fields, so you can say Sulochana Kale, Nagpur, homemaker and keep listening. There is also a mode that asks one question at a time — birth year, place, occupation — for the gaps it can see, which is far easier than facing a blank page.

Write down the uncertain things as uncertain. c. 1926, Bombay, we think. A tree full of honest approximations is worth more than a tidy one that quietly invents.

Adopted, fostered and step-children

Say so, and show it. A register has always drawn a child who joined the family another way with a broken line — it is not a slight, it is a record. Heirloom gives every child a button saying how they joined, and a person who belongs to two families is shown in both, with the second named for what it is.

3. Do not retype what already exists

If anyone in the family has already built a tree on Ancestry, FamilySearch, MyHeritage or Gramps, ask them to export a GEDCOM — a .ged file, which every genealogy program on earth can write. Import that and four hundred people arrive at once, with their dates, places, occupations, notes, marriages, divorces and adoptions.

If instead the family is scattered across three countries, send round a spreadsheet: one row per person, only a name required. Heirloom exports a blank one with the columns set up, and reads it back without duplicating anybody.

4. Choose the picture before the paper

The same family looks like several different things depending on how it is drawn, and the one you want depends on who it is for.

Then the size. As a rule of thumb: A3 for a handout, A2 for something framed, A1 for a wall once you are past thirty or forty people, and a tiled mural or a flex banner for a hall at a wedding or a gathering. A chart that is much wider than it is tall belongs on a landscape sheet — Heirloom turns the paper itself, rather than shrinking the family into a postage stamp.

5. Send the print shop a file it cannot get wrong

This is where most home-made family trees fall over. A screenshot is not a print file, and a PDF saved out of a browser's print dialog carries whatever margins and scale that dialog happened to be set to. The shop cannot tell what you meant, so it guesses.

What a press actually wants is plain:

  • The exact finished size written into the file, not a note in an email.
  • Bleed — a few millimetres of artwork past the edge, so a trim that lands a hair off does not leave a white sliver.
  • Trim marks saying where the cut goes.
  • Resolution that holds up at arm's length: 300 dpi for paper, and 100 to 150 dpi is plenty for a banner seen from across a room.

Heirloom writes that PDF itself rather than leaving it to the browser, so the file you hand over is already the right size with the marks in place. For a flex banner it will also write the file at 1:10, which is what most shops would rather receive.

A word about scripts. Devanagari cannot be set down letter by letter — the matras move and the conjuncts join — so the text in the PDF is placed as high-resolution artwork rather than as loose type. It means the Hindi and Marathi names come out exactly as they are written, which on an heirloom matters more than being able to select the words.

6. Give it away while everyone is in one room

A poster reaches the people who visit the house it hangs in. The rest of the family gets more out of things they can keep on a phone:

  • A living page — one HTML file, small enough to send on WhatsApp, that opens with no app and no internet. Tap anyone and read their life; step through to their parents and children.
  • A short film of the tree filling in, generation by generation.
  • Story cards — one square for one person, with their photograph, their years, and who they belonged to. These are what people actually post on a birthday or on the day someone is remembered.
  • A QR code printed on the poster itself, so a guest at the gathering can scan the wall and walk away with the tree.

And ask for corrections. Somebody will know a date you do not, and the fastest way to finish a family tree is to be publicly slightly wrong in front of your relatives.

Common questions

Is it really free?

Yes. Building the tree, printing it, the PDF, the images and the living page are all free, and there is no account to make.

Is my family's data uploaded anywhere?

No. It lives in your own browser as you type, because there is no server behind this. Nothing leaves unless you choose to share a link, send an order or save a file. The privacy page says exactly what travels and what does not.

Can I import a GEDCOM?

Yes — and export one. Ancestry, FamilySearch, MyHeritage and Gramps all read and write the format, so nothing you build here is trapped here.

What size should a family tree poster be?

A3 for a handout, A2 framed, A1 for a wall once you pass thirty or forty people, and a tiled mural or flex banner for a hall.

Does it write Hindi and Marathi names?

Yes, in Devanagari, on screen and in print — and you can speak the names instead of typing them.

How do I show an adopted child?

With a broken line, the way a register does. Each child carries a button saying how they joined the family, and someone who belongs to two families appears in both.
